# A polynomial extension for pure labels of bipartite perfect matchings

## Statement and consequence

Let `G` be a finite bipartite multigraph with `n` vertices on each side, `q` edges counted with multiplicity, and a perfect matching. There is a polytope `U(G)` depending only on `G`, with `O(n(q+1)³)` inequalities, and one point `u(M)` per perfect matching `M` of `G`, with the following properties.

1. `U(G)=conv{u(M)}` and edge-incidence vectors of `M` can be read linearly from `u(M)`.
2. Let `X,Y` be sets of individual edges such that every perfect matching meets exactly one of the two sets, with at least one edge from that set. Multiple occurrences from the same set are permitted. The indicator `f(M)=1[M meets X]` is an affine function of `u(M)`. The same `U(G)` works simultaneously for every such pair `X,Y`.

Consequently, a monotone projection of a permanent of polynomial order over nonnegative reals cannot agree on all Boolean inputs with the perfect matching counting polynomial of a complete undirected graph or the directed Hamiltonian cycle counting polynomial. This includes monotone affine substitutions with polynomially many nonzero input slots. It addresses the two formal counting cases stated as Open Question 5.2 in Grochow's 2017 article. The result and attribution are research claims subject to independent review. The argument below uses positivity of coefficients and recovery of multilinear coefficients from real Boolean-corner values. Boolean semiring decision equality alone does not recover those coefficients. [Grochow, *Monotone Projection Lower Bounds from Extended Formulation Lower Bounds*, pp. 9–10](https://toc.cs.uchicago.edu/articles/v013a018/v013a018.pdf).

## Connected factors from Hall's condition

Delete edges that occur in no perfect matching. This preserves every perfect matching and any associated label test. Separate connected components, each of which is balanced and has a perfect matching. First consider a connected resulting graph. If `S` is nonempty and strictly smaller than the left side, then `|N(S)|≥|S|+1`. Indeed Hall gives `|N(S)|≥|S|`. At equality every perfect matching uses all vertices of `N(S)` to match `S`, so an edge from some vertex outside `S` to `N(S)` cannot be used. No such edge remains, and no edge joins `S` to the complementary right vertices, contradicting connectedness.

If `n≥4`, either

`|N(S)| ≥ |S|+2  for every 1≤|S|≤n−2`,

or there is a set `S` of size `k` in that range with `|N(S)|=k+1`. In the second case put `W=S∪N(S)`. Every perfect matching uses exactly one edge crossing from the outside left vertices to `N(S)`. No other type of crossing edge exists. Construct `G₁` on `W` with a dummy left vertex representing the outside, retaining a separate edge for each crossing edge to its original right endpoint. Construct `G₂` on the outside with a dummy right vertex representing `W`, analogously retaining each crossing edge with its original identity. All interior edges of the corresponding shore are retained. The two graphs have `k+1` and `n−k` vertices per side. Their sizes are strictly below `n` and at least two. They are connected (they are graph contractions after discarding loops) and every retained edge remains in a perfect matching. Every perfect matching of `G` corresponds bijectively to a pair of perfect matchings of `G₁,G₂` selecting the same crossing edge `e`. Gluing such a pair uses `e` once. Every possible crossing edge occurs in at least one such pair since unusable edges were removed.

Iterating terminates at size at most three or at a graph satisfying the displayed stronger Hall condition. In a split, `(n₁−1)+(n₂−1)=n−1`. Starting with the connected components, this gives at most `n` leaves, including possible size-one components. Every edge in each factor retains a distinct original edge identifier within that factor; each factor thus has at most `q` edges. We use the construction directly, without needing the uniqueness theorem for the classical tight-cut decomposition.

## The larger indecomposable factors

Suppose `n≥4` and the stronger Hall condition holds. Any two vertex-disjoint edges extend to a perfect matching. After deleting their two left and two right endpoints, every remaining nonempty set of left vertices had at least two surplus neighbors and lost at most two, so Hall applies. Each left vertex has at least three distinct neighbors. Two specified left vertices have at least four distinct neighbors between them.

For pure `X,Y`, every edge of `X` meets every edge of `Y` physically: two vertex-disjoint such edges would extend to a perfect matching meeting both. If no two edges of `X` are vertex-disjoint, `f(M)` is exactly the sum of the individual incidence coordinates on `X`. If no two edges of `Y` are vertex-disjoint, `f(M)` is one minus that sum on `Y`.

Otherwise choose disjoint edges `a₁b₁,a₂b₂` from `X`. Each edge of `Y` must use the pair of endpoints `a₁b₂` or `a₂b₁`. Both types must occur because `Y` also contains two vertex-disjoint edges. By the same argument in reverse, every `X`-edge has endpoints `a₁b₁` or `a₂b₂`. These four cells cannot meet all perfect matchings. Each of `a₁,a₂` has a neighbor outside `{b₁,b₂}`, and their union of outside neighbors has size at least two. Choose two distinct such neighbors, one for each left vertex. The two chosen edges extend to a perfect matching and exclude all four cells. This contradicts purity. The proof allows parallel physical edges in every cell.

Use the usual doubly stochastic edge formulation with one coordinate per edge, nonnegativity and unit sums at every vertex. Its integral vertices are the perfect matchings, also for multigraphs: repeatedly extract a perfect matching from the positive support of a fractional point using Hall, then subtract its least used weight and renormalize the remainder. Thus each larger leaf uses at most `q` inequalities. For a leaf of size at most three use the simplex on its full perfect matchings. It has at most `(q+1)³` coordinates, since a perfect matching selects one edge at every left vertex. Both kinds of leaves recover all ordinary edge incidences linearly and make every pure observable affine.

## Gluing and the rectangle identity

In a split, crossing-edge incidence vectors of each factor point give a point of the simplex on possible crossing edges. Glue the two existing lifts by equating these incidence vectors. The result is exactly the convex hull of the pairs `(u₁(M₁),u₂(M₂))` that agree on their crossing choice. To see this, represent the two factor points as distributions on their matchings. Equality gives a common distribution on `e`. Conditional on each `e` of positive mass, take the product of the two conditional distributions. These compatible pairs realize the prescribed marginals. This construction adds equations and no inequalities. Every original edge incidence is read from either appropriate factor. For disconnected graphs take the ordinary product, equivalently use a single uninformative interface value. Summing over leaves proves the stated size bound.

It remains to show the simultaneous affine property. Write `f(u,v)` for the indicator on compatible integral factor matchings using crossing edge `e`. For each usable `e` choose once and for all a pair `u_e,v_e` using it. Then

`f(u,v) = f(u,v_e) + f(u_e,v) − f(u_e,v_e)`.

Indeed the two pairs on either diagonal of this rectangle use exactly the same multiset of physical edges in total (the repeated crossing coordinate is counted once per glued matching). The set of colors seen by two matchings therefore agrees on both diagonals. If both matchings on one diagonal have the same color, only that color occurs on the other diagonal. If they have different colors, the other diagonal must contain both, since every matching is pure. Either way the number of ones on both diagonals agrees.

The function `u↦f(u,v_e)` is itself a pure observable on `G₁`, where `e` is determined by `u`. Retain the color marks on side-one edges, and additionally mark the crossing edge `e` with each color that occurs in the fixed partner `v_e`. A matching of `G₁` then sees exactly the colors of its glued global matching. The same argument applies to `v↦f(u_e,v)`. Hence both terms are affine by the inductive hypothesis, using the already fixed lifts. The remaining correction is a function solely of `e`, equal to the linear combination of crossing-edge indicators with coefficients `f(u_e,v_e)`. In a product of disconnected components the correction is constant; any color contributed by a reference matching may be marked on all edges incident to one vertex of its partner component, so that every partner sees it. Induction proves that `f` is affine on the common lift.

## Recovering the target polytope from exact corner counts

For a monotone affine permanent projection, expand each matrix entry into positive scalar and variable terms. Make a parallel bipartite edge for each nonzero term. A source perfect matching has strictly positive weight and labels a monomial; a constant edge carries no variable label. The support of that monomial is the set of its edge labels, ignoring multiplicities. For simple projections the graph has at most the square of the oracle order many edges.

On the real Boolean cube, multilinearization replaces each positive exponent by one without changing values, and a multilinear polynomial is uniquely determined by these values (Boolean-lattice Möbius inversion). Therefore exact corner equality and nonnegative coefficients imply that the label set of *every* source perfect matching is a genuine target witness, and that every target witness occurs. This step is where numerical corner counting is used.

For complete-graph perfect matchings, fix a target edge `e` and one endpoint `v`. Let `X` be the source edges carrying label `e`; let `Y` be the source edges carrying any of the other edges incident to `v`. A genuine target matching contains exactly one edge incident to `v`, hence `X,Y` give a pure observable. For directed Hamiltonian cycles, take the tail of `e` and all other outgoing target edges at that tail. The same conclusion holds. All target-coordinate indicators are therefore affine functions on the single polynomial-size `U(G)`. Their joint affine map sends its vertices onto precisely the target witness incidence vectors; taking convex hull gives an extension of the target polytope.

Rothvoß proves `2^Ω(m)` extension complexity for the perfect matching polytope on `m` vertices and for the traveling-salesperson polytope. Forgetting arc orientations projects the directed Hamiltonian cycle polytope onto the undirected tour polytope by adding opposite arc coordinates. Thus the same exponential lower bound applies to a directed-cycle target. Since `U(G)` has complexity `O(n(q+1)³)`, its source cannot have polynomial size; for simple projections with `q≤n²` this also forces `n ≥ 2^Ω(m)`. [Rothvoß, *The matching polytope has exponential extension complexity*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.2369).

## The three-by-three obstruction to an affine map on the original polytope

The six `3×3` permutations consist of three even and three odd. Each physical cell appears in one even and one odd permutation, and the nine pairs exhaust `K₃,₃` between their parity classes. Let `A` contain two even and one odd permutation, with the remaining three in `B`. Label the two cells internal to `A` by `x`, the two internal to `B` by `y`, and all five remaining cells by one. The polynomial is `2x+x²+2y+y²`, equal to `3(x+y)` on Boolean inputs. A forced constant factor `1/3` gives exact counts. In particular, both color fibers are exposed faces. Their indicator fails to be affine on the original matching polytope: summing the three even permutation matrices gives the same all-ones matrix as summing the three odd ones, whereas the indicator sums differ. Replacing this small factor by its six-vertex simplex supplies the missing degree of freedom in `U(G)`.


The recursive graph operation above is the classical tight-cut decomposition. The elementary Hall-surplus proof supplies the decomposition and bounds needed here; no uniqueness of the decomposition is required. A primary account of tight cuts and braces is [de Carvalho, Lucchesi and Murty, *Thin Edges in Braces*](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/v22i4p14/pdf).

## Verification and related work

`check_pure_lifts.py` computes every nonempty physical perfect-matching configuration in the indicated finite test graphs. Bitset unions enumerate all OR realizations of colors and test whether their complements are also OR realizations. Gaussian elimination over exact rationals tests each color indicator against the common lift prescribed by the Hall construction. The source set includes every simple bipartite graph through `4×4` up to independent row and column permutations, every `2×2` matrix of edge multiplicities in `{0,1,2}`, single duplicated edges on the `3×3` representatives, and additional sparse `4×4` through `6×6` multigraphs. The run checked 4,768 pure observables on 331 sources admitting a matching. These finite results supplement the general argument.

An equivalent interpretation identifies `X`/`Y` with complementary exposed faces. A face of a bipartite perfect matching polytope is specified by coordinate vanishings. Every complementary face split thus yields two OR-mark sets. Quadratic trades preserve pure observables, but quadratic generation is an unnecessary stronger condition: Heawood is a Pfaffian cubic brace with every two-factor Hamiltonian. The elementary finite script enumerates its 24 matchings and eight distinct unordered factorizations, each isolated under two-color changes. Mori, Motomura, Ohsugi and Tsuchiya characterize the degree of the perfect-matching toric ideal via regular edge-coloring changes in Proposition 6.8; their odd-subdivision characterization in Theorem 1.4 concerns all-matchings polytopes. [Gorsky, Johanni and Wiederrecht, *A note on the 2-factor Hamiltonicity Conjecture*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08128). [Mori et al., *Toric ideals of matching polytopes and edge colorings*](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19209v1).

A smaller lift is available for the small factors if needed. First select their underlying cell permutation (at most six possibilities); conditioned on those cells the individual parallel-edge choices form a product of simplices. A pure nonconstant binary observable on such a product depends on a single cell. Indeed, changing a coordinate in a path of single-coordinate changes that reverses the color requires that cell to offer both colors; every other simultaneously selected cell must then always be unmarked. Thus the observable is affine in each fixed-permutation product and is represented by a disjunctive lift of the at most six products. This gives `O(q)` inequalities per leaf and `O(n(q+1))` overall. The simpler simplex bound above suffices for the exponential lower bound.
