# High-girth subcubic bipartite matching polynomials

Let PERM_m(X) denote the permanent of an m by m matrix of independent variables. For each m ≥ 2 and h ≥ 0 there is an explicit bipartite graph G(m,h), of maximum degree three, with a variable on every edge. Its perfect-matching generating polynomial projects to PERM_m using only the substitutions z_e = 1 and z_e = X_ij. Each X_ij occurs in the substitution exactly once. The graph has

    N = 2m(2m−1) + 2hm² vertices,    E = 4m(m−1) + (2h+1)m² edges,
    girth = 4(2h+3),                  E−N+1 = (m−1)².

## Construction and matching correspondence

For row i take a path alternating m ports indexed by columns and m−1 intervening vertices. For column j take a disjoint path alternating m ports indexed by rows and m−1 intervening vertices. Join row-i port j to column-j port i by a path of 2h+1 edges. The interiors of those joining paths are pairwise disjoint. Color row ports black and column ports white. The internal colors on each path alternate. The original ports have at most two edges along their own path and one joining edge. All other vertices have degree two.

In any perfect matching the selected edges along a joining path must alternate, since every interior vertex has to be matched exactly once. Its odd length makes its two endpoint edges agree on membership, with both selected or both unselected. Call the join active in the first case. In every row gadget all m−1 intervening vertices must match distinct ports, leaving exactly one of the m ports for an active join. The column gadgets impose the same rule. Active joins are therefore a permutation.

Conversely each permutation specifies one active join at each gadget. Removing its chosen port splits its internal path into two paths with an even number of vertices, each having a unique perfect matching. There is exactly one alternating matching pattern for each joining path with its specified endpoint status. This proves a bijection. Give the first edge of join (i,j) weight X_ij and every other edge weight 1. The matching associated with permutation σ has weight ∏_i X_i,σ(i). The identity is over the natural-number polynomial semiring and hence over every commutative semiring. Allowing independent edge variables before substitution gives the stated projection.

Every cycle uses a joining path, since the individual gadgets are trees. A cycle traversing a join takes its entire length. After each join it crosses a gadget between two distinct ports, which costs at least two more edges. Contracting the gadgets and joining paths gives a closed bipartite trail in K_m,m with no repeated edges; there is only one join for each row-column pair. The trail has at least four edges. Two consecutive rows and two consecutive columns form a cycle attaining the lower bound, proving the girth formula. Contracting the connected gadgets and joining paths exhibits K_m,m as a minor.

## Circuit consequences

Each input substitution takes a leaf to a leaf. Thus an arithmetic straight-line circuit of size s for the generic generating polynomial yields a circuit of the same size for PERM_m. This includes the leaf-counting model in Anthropic’s Statement.lean. The same transfer applies to Laurent-series approximation. Replace the variables by the same coefficients 1 and the selected input variables inside the Laurent circuit. Negative coefficient orders remain zero and reduction at order zero commutes with the substitutions. For each output monomial only a finite sum of input coefficients is involved.

In characteristic different from 2, the released VP/VNP theorem therefore gives a strict lower bound of 2^(c (log₂ m)^(4/3)) for both sizes at all sufficiently large m in each characteristic. The source proof permits c = 1/1600². Over characteristic 2 the matching polynomial of any bipartite graph is a determinant, so the projection identity survives but the lower bound does not transfer.

Taking h = m^K for fixed K gives N = Θ(m^(K+2)) and girth Θ(m^K). For every fixed 0 < ε < 1, choose K > 2(1−ε)/ε. These explicit subcubic families have girth at least N^(1−ε) for sufficiently large N, and the two circuit lower bounds still have the form 2^(Ω_ε((log N)^(4/3))). The construction and projection are polynomial-size in m for these choices.

Alternatively choose h = ceil(2^((log₂ m)^(7/6))). Then log N = Θ((log m)^(7/6)), girth is at least N/2^(O((log N)^(6/7))), and both sizes exceed 2^(Ω((log N)^(8/7))). This last family is indexed here by the resulting graph order along a subsequence. Building it from the dense dimension m costs more than polynomial in m. Its quantitative lower bound follows directly from the projection and count of vertices.

## Provenance

Valiant already gives a weighted degree-three reduction in the proof of Proposition 8 of *Completeness classes in algebra* (1979, pp. 256–257). He proves completeness for an explicit family of subcubic bipartite matching polynomials in characteristic different from two, then embeds their edges as odd-length paths to prove completeness for a two-layer rectangular lattice. His final slab has four-cycles. The fixed port graph, girth analysis, cubic completion, Lean certification and numerical exact/Laurent bounds below specify the content of the present combination. [Valiant, Proposition 8](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~billey/colombia/references/valiant.completeness.1979.pdf).

Degree reduction for exact perfect matching counts is also classical. Dagum and Luby describe a one-to-one reduction to bipartite graphs of degrees two and three, crediting Dahlhaus and Karpinski, in “Approximating the permanent of graphs with large factors” (Theoretical Computer Science, 1992). Our weighted identity tracks every original variable and combines the old gadget with odd subdivisions and the newly released exact and border algebraic lower bound. The gadget and its classical counting hardness are older results. The comparison concerns exact algebraic circuits and formal Laurent approximation, not randomized numerical approximations to positive permanents.

## A genuinely 3-regular family of logarithmic girth

A second construction produces connected **3-regular** bipartite graphs with the same exact and border lower bounds and girth at least log₂ N−O(1). We call the original edges protected. The graph remains bipartite and every protected edge survives. The construction is deterministic in time polynomial in its output size; local adjacency queries need not be computable in logarithmic time.

Let a balanced bipartite base graph H have n vertices on each side, maximum degree at most three, girth at least g ≥ 3, and positive degree deficit D=3n−|E(H)| on each side. Start with t=ceil(16·2^g/D) disjoint copies. Maintain a bipartite graph Y of maximum degree three and girth at least g by adding and switching unprotected edges. The initial deficit in each color is tD and the current deficit is tD−f if f unprotected edges have been added.

Choose a deficient vertex u on the left. If a deficient right vertex v is at distance at least g−1 from u, insert uv. Vertices in different components have infinite distance. Otherwise all deficient right vertices lie in the ball of radius g−2 around u. In a degree-three graph a ball of radius j has size at most 3·2^j−2. The total remaining deficit is at most 3(3·2^(g−2)−2), so f exceeds 13·2^g. Choose any deficient right vertex v. The union of the two balls of radius g−1 around u and v meets fewer than 9·2^g edges: there are fewer than 6·2^(g−1) vertices and each has degree at most three. Hence some unprotected edge xy, x on the left and y on the right, has both endpoints outside those balls. Delete xy and add uy and xv.

Both additions are new edges. Relative to Y−xy, each of u,v is at distance at least g from each of x,y, and x and y are at distance at least g−1 from one another, since an alternate shorter path would have made a short cycle in Y. A newly created cycle using exactly one new edge has length at least g+1. A simple cycle using both pairs paths either between u and x and between v and y, or between u and v and between x and y. The first pairing crosses a far pair and the second uses a path of length at least g−1 between x and y. Thus all cycles still have length at least g. The number of unprotected edges has increased by one, all protected edges survive, and both deficits drop by one. Repeated insertion or switching gives a cubic bipartite graph on 2tn vertices. Numbered vertices and lexicographic choices, with ordinary breadth-first search, give a deterministic construction.

Apply this to H=G(m,g) above with g=3 ceil(log₂m). Here n=(g+2)m²−m and D=(g+1)m²+m, so n≤2D. The order satisfies 2tn ≤ 2n+64·2^g. Since n=O(m²log m) and 2^g ≥ m³, this is O(2^g). Consequently g≥log₂N−O(1) and N is polynomial in m. For the projection, set every unprotected edge variable to zero. In one distinguished copy use the selector/join projection. In every other copy keep the edges of its canonical identity-permutation matching with weight one and set its other edge variables to zero. All surviving monomials come from the distinguished copy. If connectedness is desired, keep its connected component; each original connected copy lies wholly in one final component. The same specialization within the retained component suffices. The input variables, degrees and bits of the construction have polynomial size in m. Valiant’s completeness of the permanent also gives VNP-completeness of these symbolic families under polynomial-size projections in characteristic other than 2.

High girth of order log N is optimal up to its multiplicative constant for genuinely cubic graphs, by the tree-ball (Moore) bound. The power-size girth in the preceding section is enabled by its vertices of degree two. Classical regular counting hardness alone does not supply an unrestricted arithmetic-circuit lower bound. Likewise the cited cubic completion theorem about a prescribed 2-factor supplies a switching method, rather than this polynomial specialization.

The completion is a version of the Erdős–Sachs–Sauer switch, described for a prescribed 2-factor by Biggs (1998), Theorem 3.2. Dagum–Luby, Theorem 6.2, proves an exact counting result for truly cubic bipartite graphs with no girth bound. Its intermediate two/three-degree graph is the port construction used here. The matching literature calls replacement of edges by odd-length paths a bisubdivision. I found no claim in these sources establishing this quantitative arithmetic-circuit lower bound for the subclass of 3-regular bipartite graphs with logarithmic girth. This search is not evidence for priority over all other papers.

[Biggs, Theorem 3.2](https://www.kurims.kyoto-u.ac.jp/EMIS/journals/EJC/Volume_5/PDF/v5i1a1.pdf). [Dahlhaus and Karpinski](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0000(92)90005-4). [Giannopoulou, Thilikos and Wiederrecht, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09348).

Schrijver’s bound guarantees at least (4/3)^(N/2) perfect matchings in every cubic bipartite graph on N vertices. Consequently, for these graph sizes retaining the unrestricted count m! would be impossible. The cubic specialization sets selected edge variables to zero and preserves exactly the surviving monomials and their multiplicities. [Schrijver, On the number of perfect matchings in regular bipartite graphs](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/2154/2154D.pdf).

## Cubic families approaching the sextet-graph girth coefficient

A direct attachment gives stronger asymptotic girth than the elementary switching construction. Let R be a connected bipartite cubic graph with Q vertices and girth g. In any regular bipartite graph the two sides have equal size. For every subset S of one side, counting the three edges at each of its vertices gives |S| ≤ |N(S)|. Hall’s theorem yields a perfect matching. Repeating on the complementary 2-regular and then 1-regular graphs partitions the edges into three disjoint perfect matchings. For any specified edge xy, this gives a perfect matching avoiding xy and a cycle through xy (use its matching together with one other). In particular R−xy is connected, and every path from x to y in R−xy has at least g−1 edges.

Take the connected balanced graph H = G(m,g) and list the D units of missing degree on either side, allowing repeated vertices when their degree deficits exceed one. Pair the two lists in any order. For every pair (u,v) attach a fresh copy of R−xy, adding uy and xv. The result is simple, connected, cubic and bipartite, with exactly N = |V(H)| + DQ vertices. A cycle either stays in H or one reservoir, or crosses a two-edge cut around a reservoir. In the last case it includes both cut edges and an x-to-y path inside the reservoir, giving length at least g+1. Thus the resulting girth is at least g. Set all cut edges to zero. In every copy retain one fixed matching avoiding xy and set the rest of its internal edges to zero. Each surviving matching of H has one surviving extension. Composing with the H projection transfers the exact and border circuit lower bounds. This specialization gives a unique surviving extension even if the original cubic graph has additional matchings.

Biggs and Hoare constructed an explicit infinite family of bipartite cubic graphs. Weiss proved that every member’s order Q and girth g satisfy log₂ Q < 3g/4+3/2, so g > (4/3)log₂Q−2. The elementary tree-ball bound also gives g = O(log Q). With D = (g+1)m²+m and |V(H)| = O(m²g), the graph order is Θ(Qm²log Q). Choose any fixed δ>0 and take m = ⌊Q^δ⌋. Then log₂N = (1+2δ)log₂Q + O(log log Q). For every ε>0 one can choose a fixed δ small enough to obtain a connected cubic bipartite family with girth at least (4/3−ε)log₂N and exact and border circuit lower bounds of 2^(Ω_ε((log₂N)^(4/3))). Both bounds hold along this sequence in each characteristic different from two. This construction uses only ordinary matching and graph operations on the explicit reservoir. It makes no claim about polynomial-time generation from arbitrary dense dimension m outside the chosen sequence. [Weiss, *Girths of bipartite sextet graphs*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02579225).

For a coefficient converging to 4/3, set m = ⌊2^((log₂Q)^(7/8))⌋. Then log₂N = log₂Q + O((log₂Q)^(7/8)), and the girth is at least (4/3)log₂N − O((log₂N)^(7/8)). The lower bound is 2^(Ω((log₂N)^(7/6))), which is superpolynomial in the graph order. The exponent tradeoff is explicit. More generally each fixed 3/4 < a < 1 gives error O((log N)^a) and lower-bound exponent 4a/3 when log₂m ≍ (log₂Q)^a.
