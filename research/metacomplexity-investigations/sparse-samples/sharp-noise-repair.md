# Sharp repair costs from the original sparse examples

Let \(H\) be a simple graph of girth at least five and \(m>0\). Give its edge positives \(\{a,b\}\) and paired negatives \(I_{ab}\), adding only a negative row \(\{u\}\) for each degree-one vertex whose unique neighbor is \(u\). Index these examples by their respective edges and leaves, retaining multiplicity when two negative rows agree. Graphs of minimum degree two, including the cubic subdivisions, use only the original paired examples. [Sparse read-budget construction](arbitrary-read-budgets.md).

**Arbitrary feasible caps.** Given a binary monotone formula with \(s\) gates that respects the prescribed read caps and misclassifies \(t\) examples, one can construct in polynomial time a formula computing the entire graph predicate, respecting the same caps, with at most \(s+3t\) gates. The coefficient three is optimal even on trees of maximum degree three with uniform read cap two.

Delete the \(b\) edge indices with an incorrect positive or an incorrect paired negative. Of these, let \(d\) be the number where both labels are wrong. Let \(u\) count errors on indexed leaf-singleton examples; then \(t=b+d+u\). Run the diagonal formula decoder on the remaining edges, obtaining owners \(T_0\). For each vertex use the rank bound from the incident good edges. Its only possible deficit is one unit at a vertex \(v\) with exactly one remaining incident edge, no owned edge and **zero occurrences in the supplied formula**. Even a single occurrence suffices to meet its claimed lower bound at residual degree one.

Let the retained edge be \(e=va\). Since the formula ignores \(v\) and accepts \(\{v,a\}\), it accepts the singleton \(\{a\}\). If \(v\) was originally a leaf, its own negative singleton example is therefore wrong. Otherwise choose any other original incident edge \(j=vw\). Because \(w\in I_e\) and the formula is monotone and rejects \(I_e\), it rejects \(\{w\}\). It ignores \(v\), so it rejects the positive row \(\{v,w\}\). But \(a\in I_j\), and the formula accepts \(\{a\}\), so it also errs on \(j\)'s negative row. Each such defect can be assigned to a doubly erroneous edge that contains it, and an edge has only two endpoints. Total rank deficit is at most \(2d+u\). Summation gives at least \(m-b+|T_0|-2d-u\) variable leaves.

Use the required set \(R=\{v:d(v)>r_v\}\). Any required vertex missing from \(T_0\) has an incident deleted edge. Otherwise, at its full original degree the off-diagonal rank is \(d(v)\), exceeding its budget. This argument applies when \(d(v)\ge2\); feasibility rules out required vertices of degree zero or one. Start with cover candidates \(T_0\). For each deleted edge add both its required endpoints, then add one endpoint of the edge if still uncovered. This adds at most two vertices per deleted edge. The resulting cover \(T\) contains \(R\). Find any globally feasible ownership using bipartite matching. The complement of \(T\) is an independent set of vertices with zero ownership demand. Reassign every edge it owns to the other endpoint. This produces a feasible orientation whose heads all lie in \(T\). Its star formula uses at most \(m+|T_0|+2b-1\le s+3b+2d+u\le s+3t\) gates.

More precisely let \(b_R\) count the deleted edge indices with both endpoints in \(R\). Cover restoration needs at most \(b+b_R\) additional vertices, so the same algorithm uses at most \(s+2t+b_R\) gates. In particular the coefficient is at most two whenever the required vertices are independent, including for unrestricted formulas on every graph in this class.

For sharpness, take an edge \(uv\), attach two branch vertices to each of \(u,v\), and attach two separate leaves to each branch vertex. The resulting tree has 13 edges, six degree-three vertices, and eight degree-one leaves. With read cap two, all six degree-three vertices must be owners; these six cover every edge. A feasible orientation assigns each branch its two leaf edges and assigns the four other branch edges to \(u,v\). The core edge can go either way. Formula optimum is \(13+6-1=18\). Delete \(uv\). Only the four branch vertices remain required, they cover every edge, and owning all edges incident to them is feasible. The residual optimum is \(12+4-1=15\). A residual optimum makes precisely one error on the original data: the positive row \(\{u,v\}\). Every original negative remains negative under edge deletion.

**Cubic three-edge subdivisions.** Apply the original paired sample to the family from the [sparse construction](sparse-formula-consistency.md) with cap two on private variables and cap two or three on old variables. Every required vertex is old, and each edge contains at most one old vertex. Cover restoration above adds at most one vertex per deleted edge, giving a proper repair of size \(s+2b+2d=s+2t\). On a cubic base with \(n\) original vertices and \(m_0\) edges there are \(6m_0\) indexed examples. Each has weight two or three; an old coordinate occurs in six rows and a private one in five. Consequently the exact minimization and monotone-NP/poly fixed-permission identities also hold with objective **gates plus two times the number of sample errors**.

The constant two is attained with cap two throughout. Delete the middle edge from just one of the subdivided base paths. All old vertices remain required and still cover all outer edges. Each intact middle edge requires one additional distinct private owner, while the damaged path needs none. Global feasibility persists by deleting that middle edge from any feasible original orientation; no required vertex loses an owned edge. The [minimum-cover/ownership identity](arbitrary-read-budgets.md) then shows that the number of owners decreases by one and the number of target edges decreases by one, so the residual best formula saves exactly two gates. Again its sole original-sample error is the missing edge's positive row.

The repair algorithm reads the formula tree and the indexed examples. It learns each retained edge owner by a single descent through the tree.
